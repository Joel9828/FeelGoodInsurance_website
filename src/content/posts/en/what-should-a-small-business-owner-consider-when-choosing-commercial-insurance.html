Choosing the right commercial insurance is an important decision for any small business. The right coverage can help minimize the financial impact of unexpected incidents, while the wrong policy may leave important risks uncovered. This guide outlines five key considerations to review when selecting business insurance, based on practical insights from insurance professionals and business owners. Evaluating these factors can help you find coverage that fits your operations, manages potential risks, and avoids paying for protection you may not actually need.

Choose a Small-Business Insurance Specialist

Small business owners should consider working with a commercial insurance provider that understands the specific challenges faced by smaller companies. The risks, budgets, and coverage requirements of a small business can be very different from those of a large corporation, so your insurance should be designed around your actual needs.

Customer service is another important factor. Choose an insurance provider that is accessible, responsive, and willing to answer your questions clearly and professionally when you need assistance.

It is also worth checking the insurer’s financial strength and reputation. Resources such as A.M. Best can provide useful information about an insurance company’s financial ratings.

Start with essential protection such as general liability insurance, but also review whether additional coverage makes sense for your business. Consider each option based on its cost and the risks it addresses. Businesses with equipment or physical property may need commercial property insurance, while companies that use vehicles for business purposes may require commercial auto coverage. Business interruption insurance may also be appropriate if an extended shutdown could create significant financial pressure.

There are many commercial insurance options available, but the goal is to choose coverage that addresses your actual exposures rather than paying for protection you do not need.

Michelle Robbins, Licensed Insurance Agent, USInsuranceAgents

Align Policies With Actual Operations

One of the first things a business owner should evaluate is whether an insurance policy accurately reflects how the company operates. Different businesses face different exposures. A restaurant, for example, has risks involving customers, employees, food service, equipment, deliveries, property, events, and potential interruptions that may not apply to a professional office.

Review the details of the policy carefully, including exclusions, deductibles, coverage limits, and the protection available if the business has to temporarily stop operating after a covered event. A lower premium does not necessarily mean better value if important risks are excluded.

Before purchasing coverage, ask clear and specific questions. Business owners should know what their policy covers, what it excludes, how the claims process works, and whether the coverage can adapt as the company expands, adds services, hires employees, or opens additional locations.

Your insurance should be built around the business you actually operate rather than simply being something you purchase to meet a requirement.

Esmerlin Lara, Founder, City Coffee & Bar

Weigh Claims Costs and Broker Service

I operate a cleaning company in San Rafael, CA, with a W-2 team, a small fleet of vehicles, and employees working in customers’ homes every day. After more than 16 years of carrying general liability and commercial auto insurance and working with different carriers, these are some of the lessons I would share with another business owner.

Make sure the policy addresses the problems your business is most likely to encounter. In my experience, the everyday exposure can be more relevant than a dramatic worst-case scenario. For my company, something like a damaged lamp or a dissatisfied customer can be a more realistic concern than a major catastrophe.

It is also important to understand how your claims history can affect future insurance costs. Small claims made within a short period can become part of your insurance history and may influence how carriers evaluate your business when you seek new coverage. Several minor claims can potentially increase premiums over time, so I have learned to consider handling smaller losses directly and reserving insurance for more significant situations.

Look beyond the quoted premium and understand the total cost for the policy term. For example, the way commercial auto premiums are distributed throughout the year can affect your business’s cash flow and accounting. Ask how payments are structured before committing to a policy.

The service you receive from your broker matters as well. A knowledgeable broker is not much help if they take several days to respond when you have an urgent vehicle issue or customer incident. Responsiveness can make a significant difference when you need assistance with your coverage.

It is also smart to request an insurance quote before purchasing a major business asset. The insurance cost associated with a particular vehicle, for example, could significantly affect the overall cost of ownership and should be considered before making the purchase.

Finally, be transparent about your workforce. If you employ people rather than relying exclusively on independent contractors, make sure the insurer knows. Your staffing structure can affect both your coverage needs and how the carrier evaluates your business risk.

Marcos De Andrade, Founder & Owner, Green Planet Cleaning Services

Match Coverage to Trade Exposures

For businesses like ours, the most important coverage is determined by where the actual risks occur, rather than simply selecting the least expensive insurance package. We regularly work on customers’ properties, installing gates and fencing at residential properties, commercial locations, and HOA common areas. Because of this, general liability coverage is an essential part of our protection.

For example, if someone trips over equipment at a job site or a fence component accidentally damages a vehicle, general liability coverage can help protect the business from the resulting financial exposure.

Workers’ compensation is another coverage I would not overlook. Fabrication and installation involve physical tasks such as cutting metal, operating welding equipment, and moving heavy gate panels. Even with appropriate safety procedures, workplace injuries can happen.

Commercial auto coverage is also important because our trucks travel between job sites every day. Business vehicles face different risks from personal vehicles, so relying on a personal auto policy may not provide the protection a trade business requires.

One mistake business owners should avoid is purchasing a standard small-business package without checking whether it actually covers the work their employees perform. Look for an insurance professional who understands your specific trade and explain the complete process of a typical job, from arriving at the site through installation and completion.

Protect Software Risks Before Claims

For many service providers and software companies, professional liability insurance, also known as errors and omissions coverage, can be an important part of their insurance program. General liability may respond to physical incidents, such as someone being injured at your office, while E&O coverage is designed for claims alleging that your professional services or software caused a client financial loss.

We operate both an agency and a CRM product, and our E&O coverage has demonstrated its value. At one point, a client claimed that a campaign adjustment we made negatively affected their search rankings. Having professional liability coverage in place before the dispute occurred provided protection that would not have been available if we had waited until after the claim.

Business owners should also pay attention to how revenue is calculated for insurance purposes. Find out whether coverage limits and premiums automatically adjust as revenue changes or whether the business needs to notify the insurer and update the policy manually. We once experienced a period of being underinsured after our revenue increased following a product launch and our policy limits were not updated.

For SaaS companies, cyber liability coverage deserves particular attention. Businesses that store or process customer information can face significant expenses following a data breach, including investigation, notification, response, and recovery costs. General liability insurance generally does not address all of these cyber-related exposures.
